Specimen — the first verse

GENESIS 1:1
בְּרֵאשִׁ֖ית בָּרָ֣א אֱלֹהִ֑ים אֵ֥ת הַשָּׁמַ֖יִם וְאֵ֥ת הָאָֽרֶץ׃
Literal
In beginning created God the heavens and the earth.
Everyday
In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.
Conversational
In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.
Recorded reasoning

בְּרֵאשִׁית is anarthrous — in beginning, not in the beginning — and the older reading takes it as a construct: when God began to create.

Both are defensible, and the choice decides whether verse 1 is the first act of creation or a title standing over the whole chapter.

The question is recorded against the verse. It has not been quietly settled.

Most translations hide the hard part.

Every translation of the Bible is a chain of decisions. A word carries three senses and one must be chosen. A clause can attach two ways. A pronoun's referent is genuinely unclear. Ordinarily those decisions are made carefully, by capable people — and then dissolved into the finished text, where the reader cannot see them, weigh them, or disagree.

Scholia keeps them. Each rendering carries a note stating what was decided and why: which sense was taken, what the grammar permitted, where the registers diverge and on what grounds. Where the evidence genuinely does not settle the matter, the question is written down and left open rather than smoothed away.

The name is the method. Scholia (σχόλια) are the notes scholars wrote in the margins of ancient manuscripts, explaining and defending why a reading is what it is.

On the name

σχόλιον, pl. σχόλια

A marginal note on a text, written by a scholar for other readers of that text.

Abbreviated SCH.

Three registers, not one compromise.

A translation usually picks a single point on the line between the words of the original and the language people actually speak, and everyone else adjusts. Scholia renders every verse three times and lets the reader choose.

Literal WORD ORDER PRESERVED

Follows the shape of the Hebrew or Greek as closely as English allows. Keeps ambiguity ambiguous rather than resolving it silently.

Everyday READ ALOUD

Plain modern English, formal enough for public reading, with nothing archaic left standing merely because it is familiar.

Conversational SPOKEN

How one person would say it to another. Where the original is already plain, this register does not manufacture a difference.

Any difference between the three must be justified in the note. A register may not drift for the sake of sounding different.

Drafted from the original languages.

Not revised from an existing English Bible. Every verse was drafted against the tagged Hebrew and Greek — lemma, morphology, and lexical range in view — using the STEP Bible tagged texts, with Strong's and Brown‑Driver‑Briggs for lexical data.

The base text is named, not blended. The New Testament follows the SBL Greek New Testament. Passages the manuscript tradition carries but the base text does not treat as original — the long ending of Mark, the woman taken in adultery, the Johannine Comma — are printed in brackets and identified, never dropped without a word and never presented as though undisputed. The Old Testament follows the Leningrad Codex, a single manuscript witness, with Ketiv/Qere carried at word level.
